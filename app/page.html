Bent tubes

Seat tube, down tube and stay bends, seen from the side.

Seat tube: bent forward near the BB to make tyre room on short chainstays. The seat angle you set stays the effective one, so your fit doesn't move. Down tube: bent up near the head tube to give the fork crown room when the bars turn. Stays: their shape seen from the side. A bow is one smooth curve, a kink is one bend at a point, and an S-wave curves one way then the other. The sideways bend round the tyre is set in Stays & tyre room.

The builder's side-view drawing shows bends only as curved lines, without numbers. Write them in your request: Match a frame drawing → Copy for my order, or Step 4 → Write my request (both include them).

Bottles & cages

Where the bottle bosses go, and whether your bottles fit and slide out.

Side view of the front triangle. Solid outline = the bottle in its cage; dashed = slid up as far as it has to go to come out. Black dots = the bosses.

    Boss positions are measured along the tube from the BB centre to the lower boss, the way the builder's drawing dimensions them; the upper one is the spacing further up. Insulated and steel bottles can't be squeezed, so a top-load cage needs about 25 mm of room above the bottle to lift it out; a side-load cage needs much less. Cage numbers are typical: measure your own cage (bottle base to the centre of the lower bolt hole) and type it in for a closer answer.

    Fork sag (riding geometry)

    What the geometry really is once you sit on the bike and the fork sinks into its sag.

    Grey = the bike standing still · colour = with you on it, fork at its sag. Both stand on the same ground with the rear wheel in the same place.

    At every sag setting

    As drawn = the numbers you typed in Geometry (frame level, fork at full length). Builder drawings are drawn static, so this is the column that matches their numbers. Standing = the bike on flat ground with no rider (the same as drawn when the fork length levels the bike). At sag = you on the bike: the fork sinks by sag % × travel, the rear of a hardtail barely moves, so the bike tips forward around the rear wheel. Head and seat angles get steeper by that tip, the BB drops, trail shrinks and the bars come down compared with the saddle. Usual hardtail sag: 15–20 % XC, 20–25 % trail, 25–30 % aggressive (your fork maker's chart has the final word). The strength simulation is not changed by this setting; the checklist's pedal-clearance row uses it.

    What wears the weakest spot out

    Lab tests (ISO 4210)

    Lab tests

    A rough estimate of how this design might do in the ISO 4210-6 frame tests that bike brands certify to. Only a real frame on a real test rig passes or fails; these numbers come from a simplified model and are best for comparing designs. Anything flagged here is a question for your builder, not a verdict. The load margin says how much harder the test could push before the model expects trouble; impact tests compare the estimated permanent bend with the allowed set.

    Seated comfort

    Seated comfort

    A 1000 N (about 100 kg) push straight down at the sit bones, through the seatpost into the frame, with the rear axle held. Stiffness in N/mm: lower is plusher. For scale, TOUR-style lab tests of whole bikes run from about 70 N/mm (flex post, very plush) to 250+ (stiff alloy post, harsh). Each part's share comes from where the bending energy goes, so the shares add up exactly.

    Comfort explorer & optimiser

    Comfort explorer

    sweeps and a comfort optimiser

    Try one change at a time, or let the optimiser search combinations for the plushest frame that still meets the rules below (model estimates; your builder has the final say). Each variant is a full re-run: ISO tests, rock hits and landings, fatigue life, stiffness.

    Rules the optimiser must keep

    Rules are relative to the design you have open where that makes sense, so a design that is already near a limit can't get worse. A variant must meet every ISO estimate and rule outright to be "within rules". If the open design already fails a rule, a variant that is no worse on it is shown as "no worse than now" (amber), never as within rules. Seatpost insertion, seat-tube fit and stiffness are always hard rules.

    What the optimiser may change

    Weld joints

    Weld joints

    Efthymiou stress-concentration factors (DNV-RP-C203)

    How much each weld multiplies the tube stress at its hottest point, from the joint's proportions: β = brace/chord diameter, γ = chord radius/wall, τ = brace wall/chord wall, θ = angle. A thin chord (seat tube, head tube) with a big brace gives a high factor; a thicker chord wall or a sleeve brings it down. Hot-spot stress is the factor applied to the worst everyday or abuse load. These textbook factors come from offshore joints and don't credit the TIG bead, so read them as a ranking of which welds work hardest.

    Stress by tube

    Stress by tube

    Peak stress in each tube for each load. Colour shows how much of the titanium's yield strength is used: under 35% is comfortable, 35–60% is working hard, over 60% is worth a question to your builder. These come from a simplified model; the builder's own analysis decides.

    CalculiX check

    CalculiX check

    checking…

    Runs the same frame and loads through CalculiX, the open-source finite-element solver, and compares it with the quick model above. Needs Cyclic Labs started with “Start Cyclic Labs” on Windows (that runs the solver that comes with it).

    3D weld models (every joint)

    Builds a solid 3D model of each welded tube joint of this design, with a real fillet weld bead, and has CalculiX measure how much the weld concentrates stress. The results are kept in this browser and used by the fatigue method "Hot-spot with 3D weld models". Takes a few minutes per joint; leave the page open.

    How to read the engineering results

    How to read this

    Each tube is a hollow titanium beam with the diameter, wall, oval shape and bends you enter, welded solid at the joints. The fork is a rigid test fixture, as in a lab frame test. It's a quick, simplified model: good for seeing how one design compares with another, not a replacement for the builder's own engineering.

    • Your builder has the final say. A good builder checks every frame with their own analysis and years of real frames, so nothing here means a design is wrong or won't work. Want more margin anyway? Ask for bigger or thicker tubes; builders will usually do it.
    • Best for comparing. "Is B stiffer, lighter, longer-lasting than A, and by how much" is what this answers well. Absolute fatigue years depend on assumptions about how often big hits happen.
    • Welds are the weak link. At every weld the titanium is softened by the heat (about 500 MPa yield instead of 725), and fatigue uses a welded-joint S-N curve (slope 3, as in IIW practice) calibrated so frames like your gravel bike pass ISO with the margin real ones have. Change it under Material.
    • Fatigue years are rough. They hinge on how often big hits happen; treat them as roughly ±10×, and use them to compare designs.
    • Riding loads. Conditions follow EN 17406 / ASTM F2043. Rock-hit sizes are based on measured mountain-bike hub loads (up to about 5× body weight on a full-suspension bike, more on a hardtail). Landings use your legs, tyres and fork as the cushion.
    • Impact tests are estimates. Once a weld yields, the program assumes the tube bends like a thin tube's plastic hinge; real frames often spread the damage and do a bit better.
    • Not modelled: gussets, the exact weld-toe shape on joints that haven't had a 3D weld model run, and local tube buckling beyond the AISC rule. Butted walls, bends and 3D weld factors (CalculiX card) are included.
    • Realistic view & animations are for looking only: they draw the same analysis, never change a number. Animations scale each analysed load up and down in time (the model is linear, so flex and stress scale with the load).

    Strength simulation: please read

    Applies to everything on the Strength (FEA) tab: stress, strain, fatigue life, lab tests, stiffness, comfort, weld joints and CalculiX.

    • It's a simulation, not an approval. Cyclic Labs is an independent hobby tool for sketching and comparing bike frame designs. Its strength results are rough estimates from a simplified computer model. They are not engineering advice, a design approval or a safety certification.
    • There may be mistakes. The results can be wrong: there may be bugs in the software, the physics is simplified, and the material strength, weld quality, riding loads and crash forces are all assumptions. Numbers typed in or read off a drawing by the scanner can be wrong too. The tool hasn't been checked by a licensed engineer or proven against real lab tests of your frame.
    • A pass doesn't mean safe, and a fail doesn't mean unsafe. Only a real frame on a real test rig passes or fails ISO 4210. Use the numbers to compare one design with another and to come up with questions, not as verdicts.
    • Always ask your frame builder or a qualified frame designer. Have them review and approve every design before it's made, and follow their advice over anything Cyclic Labs says. A good builder checks frames with their own analysis and experience from many real frames.
    • Don't rely on it for safety decisions, such as whether a frame is safe to ride, how much weight it can carry, or whether a damaged, modified or repaired frame is OK to use.
